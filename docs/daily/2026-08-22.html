    Superconductivity was recently observed in twisted WSe₂ and MoTe₂, raising a central question: is the pairing driven by electronic correlations, by phonons, or by both? Answering it requires determining the electron--phonon coupling (EPC) in these moiré semiconductors, whose calculation in realistic supercells of thousands of atoms lies beyond the reach of direct first-principles methods. Here we combine filling-dependent Raman spectroscopy with machine-learning first-principles calculations to obtain the EPC mode by mode in supercells of up to tens of thousands of atoms. Raman reveals only a few moiré phonons whose frequencies shift strongly with filling; we trace this to an interference selection rule: a phonon couples strongly only when its displacement texture matches the static lattice-reconstruction pattern, and is otherwise suppressed by destructive interference. The rule selects the low- and high-frequency breathing modes seen in Raman and makes the coupling peak at large twist angles, near those at which superconductivity appears. Lattice-reconstruction interference thus emerges as an organizing principle for moiré EPC, pointing to a substantial, potentially dominant, phonon contribution to large-angle pairing.

    📖 英文原文

    Dust storms are a fundamental Earth system process, influencing global climate, biogeochemical cycles, and human health. However, how a changing climate reshapes their activity remains largely uncertain. Here we show that while externally forced long-term warming tends to suppress the global frequency of dust storms, increased internal climate variability, manifesting as greater temperature fluctuations, paradoxically enhances the spring dust storm intensity. Simply put, the greater the temperature variability during the preceding winter, the stronger the ensuing spring dust storm activity. We resolve this paradox by identifying a cross-seasonal soil-memory effect driven by winter minimum temperature variability. Increased temperature variability in the preceding winter dries out deeper soil layers, leaving a persistent, highly erodible surface primed for intense dust emission in the subsequent spring. This soil-memory pathway forms a physical bridge, teleconnecting winter thermal anomalies over the North Atlantic to spring dust activity across global source regions. A machine-learning model, leveraging these winter precursors, predicts spring dust intensity with unprecedented skill, thereby independently validating this physical framework. Our findings thus establish preceding-winter minimum temperature variability as a critical, previously overlooked driver of spring dust storms, offering a new framework for seasonal predictability of terrestrial processes in the Earth system.
