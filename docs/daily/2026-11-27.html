    Antimicrobial resistance has increased the need for new antibacterial agents and scalable production methods. Cliotide U1 (CU1), a plant-derived antimicrobial peptide, has reported activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, but its production has relied on low-yield extraction or chemical synthesis. Here, CU1 was recombinantly expressed in Pichia pastoris, and the antibacterial activity and properties of the CU1-containing fermentation supernatant (CU1-FS) were characterized. CU1-FS inhibited all six tested bacterial strains, with apparent minimum inhibitory concentrations of 3–8 µg/mL based on ELISA-estimated His-tagged products. Antibacterial activity was retained to varying degrees after temperature, pH, salt, and protease treatments, and CU1-FS showed low hemolysis of human erythrocytes under the tested conditions. Scanning electron microscopy showed changes in bacterial cell morphology; propidium iodide staining and measurements of extracellular nucleic acid- and protein-associated absorbance supported increased membrane permeability and leakage of intracellular components. Agarose gel electrophoresis showed altered migration of bacterial genomic DNA, consistent with a potential in vitro interaction. Molecular docking and 100-ns molecular dynamics simulations provided predicted interaction models for CU1 with selected bacterial proteins and a model lipid membrane. Because the tested material was unpurified CU1-FS, contributions from other supernatant components cannot be excluded. These findings establish a recombinant production approach for bioactive CU1-containing supernatant and support further study of CU1-associated membrane effects.

    Repeated drying-wetting and freeze-thaw cycles severely deteriorate ancient masonry structures, threatening the long-term conservation of architectural heritage. This work provides a sustainable and extensible reinforcement strategy for the engineering conservation of ancient masonry buildings.
